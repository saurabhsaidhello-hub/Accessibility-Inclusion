POUR principles
( What is it )

Accessible design isn't a feature you add at the end. It's the floor plan — the ramps, wide doors, and clear signage that let everyone use the building.

If visual design is the paint and the furniture, accessibility is the structure underneath: the part that decides who can actually get in and move around.

Same doorway, different defaults
( Four core principles — POUR )
01
Perceivable
Can people sense it?
Information must be presentable in ways users can perceive — text alternatives, captions, sufficient contrast. Content that only exists as color, sound, or a faint image excludes someone.
02
Operable
Can people use it?
Interface components must be operable — keyboard accessible, no seizure-inducing content, enough time to read or use content. If it only works with a precise mouse gesture, it doesn't work for everyone.
03
Understandable
Can people follow it?
Information and UI operation must be understandable — readable text, predictable behavior, input assistance. Errors should say what's wrong and how to fix it, not just that something broke.
04
Robust
Does it hold up?
Content must work with a wide range of user agents, including assistive technologies — valid, semantic code. A screen reader should announce "button," not "div."
( The legal backbone )

Almost every accessibility law points to the same technical spec. The law decides your deadline and your risk. WCAG 2.1 Level AA decides what "accessible" actually means.

The law sets the deadline. WCAG sets the bar.
( WCAG 2.1 Level AA )

WCAG — the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines — is published by the W3C and split into three conformance levels: A (minimum, prevents the most severe barriers), AA (the level nearly every law references), and AAA (highest, rarely required site-wide). Meeting AA means satisfying all 30 Level A criteria plus 20 additional Level AA criteria. Most of what a designer is actually responsible for — as opposed to a developer — lives in a handful of those 20.

01
1.4.3 — Contrast (Minimum)
The one everyone already knows
Normal text needs at least 4.5:1 contrast against its background; large text (18pt+, or 14pt+ bold) needs 3:1. The criterion designers reach for first — and the one most design systems still get wrong on secondary or disabled-looking text.
02
1.4.11 — Non-text Contrast
Icons, borders, inputs
UI components and graphical objects — icon strokes, input borders, toggle states — need at least 3:1 contrast against their surroundings. This is the one that gets missed when a design leans on faint 1px borders.
03
1.4.4 & 1.4.10 — Resize Text & Reflow
Zoom to 200%, zoom to 400%
Text must scale up to 200% without losing content or function, and the whole layout must reflow to a single column at 400% zoom without needing to scroll horizontally. Fixed-width containers and text that gets clipped are the usual failure.
04
2.4.7 — Focus Visible
Not just the browser default
Every interactive element needs a visible indicator when it has keyboard focus. Designers often strip the default outline for aesthetics without designing a replacement — which is the single most common AA failure in otherwise polished interfaces.
05
2.4.6 — Headings and Labels
Describe, don't decorate
Headings and form labels must describe their topic or purpose — "Email address," not just a placeholder that disappears once someone starts typing.
06
3.3.3 — Error Suggestion
Say what to fix, not just that it's broken
When a form input fails validation, the error message must suggest how to fix it. "Invalid input" fails this; "Enter a date in the form DD/MM/YYYY" passes.
( Who it applies to )
01
ADA Title II — US
State & local government
A 2024 DOJ rule requires public entities' websites and apps to meet WCAG 2.1 A and AA. The compliance deadline has since moved to April 2027 for larger entities and April 2028 for smaller ones — the standard itself hasn't changed.
02
ADA Title III — US
Private business
No formal technical rule — enforced through case law, with Domino's v. Robles as the landmark. Courts treat WCAG 2.1 AA as the de facto standard anyway. There's no fixed deadline, just litigation risk.
03
Section 508 — US
Federal agencies & contractors
Explicitly ties to WCAG 2.0 AA, with many agencies now holding themselves to 2.1 in practice.
04
European Accessibility Act
EU private sector
In force since June 2025. Casts a wider private-sector net than current US law — e-commerce, banking apps, ebooks, transport apps.
( Indian guidelines )

India runs its own track — one statute rather than court precedent, with its own technical standard layered on top of WCAG.

01
RPwD Act, 2016
Sections 40–46 · the core law
Requires government bodies and private service providers to meet accessibility standards for digital platforms. Non-compliance draws a fine starting at ₹10,000 for a first offence, rising up to ₹5,00,000 for repeat violations — there's no single fixed nationwide deadline like the US Title II date.
02
IS 17802
India's ICT accessibility standard
India's own standard, built directly on WCAG 2.1 AA with added specifics for government platforms and universal design in electronic goods — the same underlying bar as everywhere else, formalised as a domestic standard.
03
GIGW 3.0
Guidelines for Indian Government Websites
The mandatory baseline for all central and state government websites and apps, aligned with WCAG 2.1 AA and audited through STQC certification.
04
Enforcement is active
CCPD, RBI, and insurance regulators
Since July 2024 the Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities (CCPD) has issued non-compliance notices to both government departments and private platforms. The RBI pushed accessible digital banking in October 2024, with similar sector guidance from insurance regulators.

India also regulates manipulative design directly, outside the accessibility track — see the Dark Patterns tab for the CCPA's separate consumer-protection guidelines.

( One takeaway )

Legal risk is usually a symptom of a design system that never built AA in as a default state.

( Four checks, from Material Design 3 )

Google's Material Design 3 turns POUR into four checks a designer can apply directly to a screen.

Text needs 4.5:1. Icons and UI components need 3:1.

The three boxes are the same check applied to three different materials. The first is light grey text on a dark background — common for "subtle" secondary text — measuring only 2.1:1, well under the 4.5:1 text minimum, so it fails. The second swaps in a near-black-on-white pairing that measures 7.4:1, comfortably clearing AA. The third isn't text at all: it's an icon's outline stroke against its box, which only needs to clear the lower 3:1 bar set for UI components and graphics, and at 3.4:1 it does. The lesson: text and interface elements are held to different thresholds, so a "passing" grey icon stroke can still be the wrong colour for grey body copy sitting right next to it.

01
Colour contrast
Text needs at least 4.5:1 against its background. Icons, borders, and other UI components need at least 3:1. Never rely on colour alone to carry meaning.
02
Structure
A logical hierarchy of headings, grouped content, and named regions lets people navigate by structure instead of scanning by eye.
03
Flow
Content should follow one predictable order for reading, tabbing, and task completion — visually and in the underlying code.
04
Elements
Interactive components need enough size, spacing, and clearly different states. Material sets a minimum touch target of 48 × 48 dp, even when the visible icon is smaller.

Zepto displayed a low price on the product listing, then added undisclosed handling fees and other charges only at the final checkout screen — after the customer had already committed time to the order.

This exhibit isn't one company — it's a signal. In 2025, the Department of Consumer Affairs convened Amazon, Apple, Airbnb, Booking.com, Samsung, Mastercard, Uber and WhatsApp specifically to discuss eliminating deceptive design, warning that dark patterns cause "unintended purchases, privacy violations, and increased digital addiction."

Why this exhibit matters

Notices to individual companies (A–D) fix one interface at a time. A roundtable with global platforms is the regulator trying to shift industry norms before the next dark pattern even ships — the difference between punishing a crime and rewriting the incentive structure that produces it.

STATUS → Guidelines remain advisory; no standard penalty schedule yet exists for repeat or novel violations.
